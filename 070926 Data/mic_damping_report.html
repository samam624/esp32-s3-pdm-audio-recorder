MEMS Mic Damping — Objective Frequency Response Study

070926 devkit bench test — 9 damping treatments × 3 stimuli (ambient background, hairdryer low, hairdryer high as a wind-tunnel proxy), analyzed from the 27 raw WAV recordings via Welch PSD, band-limited RMS, clipping, and crest-factor metrics.

⚠ Data-integrity correction applied before analysis

The raw RMS levels didn't match the spreadsheet's row labels for 7 of the 9 conditions: files logged as "background" were often louder than the paired "hairdryer (L)" file for the same condition — physically backwards, since background (no stimulus) should always be the quietest of the three.

Checked against each file's own spectral fingerprint (true silence sits 20–30 dB below any hairdryer take, with a sharp, unambiguous gap — not a judgment call), the pattern is a consistent one-slot rotation: for every condition after the first two tested ("lid on", "scotch tape"), the file logged as background was actually recorded during hairdryer-high, the file logged as hairdryer-L was actually the true silent background, and the file logged as hairdryer-H was actually hairdryer-low. This looks like the physical test order got shifted by one step relative to how the spreadsheet columns were filled in, starting partway through the session.

All charts and numbers below use the content-verified labels, not the sheet's literal row order. Worth checking your bench notes from that day against this — if the actual protocol was genuinely different, the mapping below may need revisiting.

1. Wind-noise attenuation (hairdryer-high vs. bare mic)

Hairdryer-high stands in for wind-tunnel turbulence — the stimulus that causes peaking. Attenuation is measured relative to the lid off condition (bare, undamped mic), in the full analysis band (100 Hz–16 kHz) and separately in the 20–150 Hz rumble band where turbulence noise concentrates. Positive = quieter than the bare mic (good). Caveat: the hairdryer was hand-held, not fixed in a jig, so absolute dB across different re-positionings (i.e. across conditions) carries some experimental noise — treat differences under ~2 dB as inconclusive.

2. Headroom: low-fan → high-fan level increase

Within each condition, low and high fan speed were recorded back-to-back at the same physical setup — so this delta is robust to hairdryer positioning drift between conditions, unlike the absolute dB above. A small delta plus a quiet low-fan reading means the treatment is genuinely blocking turbulence, not just measured differently; a small delta with a loud low-fan reading means it's already leaking heavily and simply has nowhere further to go.

3. Frequency-response coloration (ambient background vs. bare mic)

Deviation of each condition's ambient-background spectrum from the bare-mic ("lid off") background spectrum, by band. Ambient room tone is a weak, uncontrolled source sitting close to the mic's self-noise floor, so this is a coarser check than the wind-attenuation numbers — useful mainly for spotting a treatment that clearly discolors the response, not for fine ranking.

4. Spectra by condition

Welch power spectral density (Hann window, 4096-pt, 50% overlap), log-frequency axis. Each panel: background (light), hairdryer-low (mid), hairdryer-high (dark) for that condition. The vertical gap between the light and dark lines is the treatment's total wind-noise headroom at a glance; a gap that barely opens up (e.g. bare "lid off") means turbulence saturates the mic even at low fan speed.
